# Distributed Practice in Verbal Recall Tasks (Cepeda et al., 2006)

**Citation:** Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin*, 132(3), 354–380.

**Link:** https://escholarship.org/content/qt3rr6q10c/qt3rr6q10c.pdf

## Summary

This is the canonical quantitative synthesis of the spacing effect: a meta-analysis of 839 assessments of distributed practice drawn from 317 experiments across 184 articles, examining how inter-study interval (ISI, the gap between study sessions) and retention interval (the gap between last study and final test) jointly determine memory retention. It found that spaced learning episodes reliably outperform massed (back-to-back) repetition, and — critically for scheduler design — that the ISI producing maximal retention increases as the desired retention interval increases, meaning the "optimal" gap between reviews is not a fixed constant but should expand as the target recall horizon lengthens. This is the empirical bedrock for Seshat's FSRS-based expanding-interval scheduling: it justifies spacing reviews apart rather than clustering them, and justifies growing intervals over time rather than using a fixed or shrinking cadence.

**Effect size:** The meta-analysis reports the spacing effect via mean proportion-correct differences and interaction effects between ISI and retention interval rather than a single pooled Cohen's d (it is a synthesis of raw recall-proportion effects across heterogeneous study designs, not a single-metric meta-analysis). A representative published contrast from the underlying data: for a retention interval of about 1 week, expected recall was highest with an ISI of roughly 1–2 days, while for a retention interval of about 1 year, optimal ISI extended to roughly 1–2 months — illustrating the retention-interval-dependent optimal-spacing relationship. Later quantitative reviews (e.g., Donoghue & Hattie, 2021, in this folder) summarize the broader distributed-practice literature at d=0.85.
