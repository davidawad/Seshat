# Making Things Hard on Yourself, but in a Good Way: Creating Desirable Difficulties to Enhance Learning

**Citation:** Bjork, E. L., & Bjork, R. A. (2011). Making Things Hard on Yourself, but in a Good Way: Creating Desirable Difficulties to Enhance Learning. In M. A. Gernsbacher, R. W. Pew, L. M. Hough, & J. R. Pomerantz (Eds.), *Psychology and the Real World: Essays Illustrating Fundamental Contributions to Society* (pp. 56–64). Worth Publishers.

**Link:** https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf

## Summary

This foundational book chapter introduces the "desirable difficulties" framework: conditions that slow or increase errors during initial learning (e.g., spacing, interleaving, and retrieval practice) but improve long-term retention and transfer, versus conditions that make performance look better in the moment (massed/blocked practice) yet produce weaker durable learning. The chapter's central methodological warning — that current performance during study is an unreliable proxy for actual learning — is the theoretical basis for why Seshat should schedule reviews using a forgetting-curve model (FSRS) rather than optimizing for immediate in-session accuracy or subjective ease.

**Effect size:** Not quantified in source bibliography (theoretical/review chapter, not an empirical study reporting a pooled effect size)
