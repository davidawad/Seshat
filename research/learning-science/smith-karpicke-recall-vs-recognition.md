# Retrieval Practice with Short-Answer, Multiple-Choice, and Hybrid Tests

**Citation:** Smith, M. A., & Karpicke, J. D. (2014). Retrieval practice with short-answer, multiple-choice, and hybrid tests. *Memory*, 22(7), 784–802.

**Link:** https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Smith_Karpicke_Memory.pdf

## Summary

Across four experiments (N=372), students practiced retrieval using short-answer, multiple-choice, or hybrid (short-answer-then-multiple-choice) question formats after reading educational texts, with retention measured a week later. The key finding was that short-answer retrieval practice did not show a reliable advantage over multiple-choice/recognition-based practice when the short-answer task's retrieval success rate was low; the advantage for short-answer/hybrid formats emerged once retrieval success improved, indicating the benefit of testing comes from successful retrieval practice generally, not from maximizing task difficulty for its own sake. This directly justifies Seshat's support for hints and difficulty-adjustment on recall-type cards: the design target should be "difficult but successful retrieval" (tuned so learners can actually succeed most of the time), not blindly forcing the hardest possible short-answer format regardless of success rate.

**Effect size:** Not quantified in source bibliography (reports condition-by-condition retention comparisons across 4 experiments rather than a single pooled effect size; benefit of testing over restudy was consistent across formats, with short-answer/hybrid advantage over pure multiple-choice contingent on retrieval success rate)
