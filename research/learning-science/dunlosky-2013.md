# Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques (Dunlosky et al., 2013)

**Citation:** Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4–58.

**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266

## Summary

This foundational monograph evaluates ten learning techniques for utility, generalizability, and ease of implementation, rating practice testing and distributed practice as "high utility" while rereading, highlighting, and summarization are rated "low utility." This directly supports Seshat's core architecture: FSRS-based distributed scheduling paired with active-recall card formats (short-answer/cloze) rather than passive re-reading, since the review found retrieval practice and spacing generalize robustly across learner ages, materials, and criterion tasks (including durable long-term retention) while most other popular techniques (highlighting, keyword mnemonics, imagery, rereading) do not. It also flags implementation constraints — e.g., students often fail to self-regulate use of high-utility techniques without external scaffolding — which is relevant to Seshat's automated (rather than user-discretionary) scheduling and confidence-calibration prompts.

**Effect size:** Not quantified as a single pooled effect size in this monograph — it is a qualitative/narrative review, not a meta-analysis, and rates techniques categorically (low/moderate/high utility) rather than reporting a summary d. Donoghue & Hattie (2021) later quantified these same ten techniques meta-analytically (see `donoghue-hattie-2021.md` in this folder): distributed practice d=0.85, practice testing d=0.74.
